The Lost Property Office

The lost property office at Harlow Junction was a single room under the stairs to platform four, and Dev Okafor had run it alone for six years.

Most of what came in was ordinary. Umbrellas, mostly, and gloves that had lost their partners somewhere between the ticket barrier and the coffee stand. Phone chargers. A surprising number of single shoes. He wrote each item in a green notebook, gave it a number on a paper tag, and put it on the shelves that ran floor to ceiling along three walls. After ninety days, anything unclaimed went to a charity shop on Mill Road. He liked the rule. It meant the room never filled up, and it meant that every object had a clock on it, whether anyone came for it or not.

On the last Thursday in November a cleaner brought in a small wooden box.

It was the size of a paperback, dark with handling, closed with a brass hook. There was no name on it. Dev turned it over twice, then opened it, because that was the rule.

Inside were train tickets. Hundreds of them, stacked on their edges like cards in a file, every one of them for the same journey: Harlow Junction to Carrow Bay, one way. The oldest were thick orange cards, the kind the railway stopped making before he was born. The newest was from last week.

He took one out and checked the date. Then another. They went back forty-one years, with no gaps longer than a month.

He wrote the box into the notebook as item 2207, "wooden box, contents tickets," and put it on the middle shelf, where he would see it from his desk.

Nobody came on Friday. Nobody came the next week.

He found himself counting the tickets in the quiet hour after the morning rush. There were four hundred and ninety-two. One way, never return. Someone had made the same trip, one way, roughly once a month, for most of a lifetime, and had kept every ticket in order.

In the second week he looked up Carrow Bay. It was a small town at the end of the coast line, with a harbour, a church, and a home for old people called Seaview.

He did not call the care home. It was not his job, and the notebook had a column for "claimed by" but no column for guesses.

On the eighty-ninth day a woman came to the window. She was perhaps seventy, in a long raincoat, and she asked, without much hope in her voice, whether anyone had handed in a small wooden box.

Dev took it down from the middle shelf and put it on the counter between them. She did not open it. She put both hands flat on the lid and stood like that for a while.

"It was my husband's," she said at last. "He went to see his brother every month. His brother has lived at Seaview since he got ill. My husband died in September. I've been going in his place."

She opened the box then, took out last week's ticket, and slid it to the front of the stack.

"I'll keep them going," she said, and signed the notebook in the column that said "claimed by," and went out to catch the 11:14.
